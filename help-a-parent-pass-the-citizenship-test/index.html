Helping a parent pass the citizenship test

If you are the one who searched for this, you are probably the one who will make the difference. Here is how to help without doing it for them.

First, know which test they face

Ask when the N-400 was filed. On or after October 20, 2025 means the 2025 test: 128 possible questions, up to 20 asked, 12 to pass. Before that date means the 2008 test: 100 questions, 10 asked, 6 to pass. Everything on this site is built for the 2025 version, which covers nearly all of the 2008 one.

Then check the exceptions. If your parent is 50 or older with 20 years as a permanent resident, or 55 with 15, they can skip the English test and take civics in their own language with an interpreter. At 65 with 20 they study only 20 questions. Many families don't know this and study far more than they need to. The rules in detail.

Set up their phone (five minutes)

  • Open the trainer on their phone and add it to the home screen (Safari: Share → Add to Home Screen; Chrome: menu → Add to Home screen). It looks and opens like an app.
  • Read-aloud is on by default. Turn up the volume and play the first card so they hear the officer's pace.
  • If they read Spanish, open the menu (☰) and turn on Español: every question and answer shows in Spanish under the English. Chinese, Vietnamese, and Arabic speakers can start from the 中文, Tiếng Việt, and العربية pages.
  • If they qualify for 65/20, turn on that switch in the same menu. The deck shrinks to the 20 questions.

How to quiz them

The most useful thing you can do is be the officer. Sit across from them, ask a question out loud in English, and wait. Don't help for the first few seconds; the silence is where the memory work happens. Then either they answer or you show them. Ten questions a night is plenty.

  • Use classroom mode on a laptop or TV: it asks the question aloud, counts down, then shows the answer. Set it to "When I tap" so you control the pace.
  • Use the practice test once a week to see where they are. Twelve right is a pass.
  • Pick one answer per question with them and stick to it. "The Constitution," not four variations. Fewer words, said the same way every time, is what holds up under nerves.
  • Type in their governor, senators, and representative on the cards that ask, and check the current President, Vice President, Speaker, and Chief Justice at uscis.gov/citizenship/testupdates the week of the interview.

The part families forget

Most people who fail don't fail civics. They fail the N-400 review, where the officer reads the application back and asks questions in English: "Have you ever been arrested?" "Have you taken any trips outside the United States?" Run the interview practice with them a few times, and go through their actual N-400 together so the answers match what was filed. If anything changed since filing, a new address, a trip, a job, bring the paperwork.

Documents and the day

Print the checklist and gather the folder with them a week early; old passports are the thing that gets forgotten. On the day: eat, arrive 20 minutes early, and know that you can't go into the interview room (unless you are their interpreter under an exemption, or their attorney). Wait outside with a phone charger and lunch plans.

Nerves

Older applicants often know the material and freeze. Two things help. Practice saying the answers to a person, not a screen, so a human face isn't new on the day. And tell them the truth about the format: the officer stops at 12 right, they can ask for a question to be repeated, and any accepted answer counts. Most interviews are over in 20 minutes and most people pass.

Last reviewed September 5, 2026. Procedures vary by field office and change over time; confirm anything that matters for your case at uscis.gov.
